Is it common sense,
or just you?

Everyone thinks that they have common sense, that everybody knows what they know, too. We put this to the test: People rate short claims, saying whether they agree and whether most others would agree as well. Their answers show which beliefs are truly shared, and who can tell.

5 · LLM evaluations

What does this imply about LLMs?

Large language models can be evaluated in the same way humans are. We tested 44 LLMs against 2,046 human individuals, asking them whether they agree with a statement and whether they predict that most people would agree with it. Since humans and LLMs performed the same task, they can be directly compared with one another.

About this score

How commonsensical is a person?

Each participant rates a set of statements and answers two questions about each one: Do you agree with it? and Do you think most other people would? Their score is high when their own answers side with the majority, and they can tell what the majority thinks.

  1. 1
    Consensus
    The share of their statements where their own answer matches the majority's.
  2. 2
    Awareness
    The share where their guess about most other people matches the majority's answer.
  3. 3
    Commonsensicality
    the square root of consensus × awareness, from 0 to 100%. This geometric mean needs both parts to be high: 100% and 25% give 50%, where a plain average would give 62.5%.

The majority is that of the group chosen in against the majority in above, over all dates: “agree” when at least 50% of it agree. A statement counts once that group has rated it 10 times, and a participant needs 5 statements that count.

About this score

How commonsensical is a statement?

A statement is common sense when nearly everyone gives the same answer to it, and expects others to give that answer too. Its score comes from everyone in the selected group who rated it.

  1. 1
    Consensus
    How lopsided the vote is: 2 × |share who agree − 50%|. It is 100% when everyone gives the same answer and 0% at an even split.
  2. 2
    Awareness
    The share of raters who predicted the majority's answer: those who said most others would agree when the majority agrees, or would not when it disagrees.
  3. 3
    Commonsensicality
    the square root of consensus × awareness, from 0 to 100%. A statement most people reject can still score high, if people expect it to be rejected.

A statement is scored once it has at least 10 ratings in the group. A 50/50 tie counts as a majority that agrees.

About design points

Which kinds of statements are most commonsensical?

Every statement is categorized in six ways: fact or opinion, literal language or figure of speech, and so on. Each of the 26 = 64 combinations of these six features is a design point. Its score is the average commonsensicality of its statements, so comparing design points shows which kinds of knowledge people share most.

  1. 1
    One row per design point
    ● marks the first side of a feature (Fact, Physical, Literal, Positive, Knowledge, Everyday) and ○ the second (Opinion, Social, Figurative, Normative, Reasoning, Abstract). Click the ? in a column header for the full definition.
  2. 2
    Average score
    The dot is the mean commonsensicality of the design point's statements, and the bar its 95% confidence interval.
  3. 3
    N and ranking
    N counts the statements with a score (at least 10 ratings). A design point needs N ≥ 5 to be ranked; the others are listed under the table.

Features overlap, and are also tied to when and where statements were collected, so read differences between design points as descriptions, not causes.

About this table

Is a statement equally commonsensical everywhere?

Each cell is one statement's commonsensicality (0–100), computed only from one country's participants, the same way as in Statement Scores. Reading along a row shows where a view is widely shared, and expected, and where it is not.

  1. 1
    Cells
    The score, with its number of ratings N underneath. A cell that shows only N has fewer than 10 ratings, too few to score. Click any cell to see the calculation.
  2. 2
    Countries, M and SD
    How many countries score the statement, and the mean and standard deviation of their scores.
  3. 3
    Order
    Rows are sorted by number of countries and columns by number of scored statements. Click a header to sort by it.

Small cells are noisy: with 10 ratings, a cell's score has a standard error of about 16 points (the median across statements), so compare cells with many ratings.

A score says how widely a country's own majority view is shared, whichever way it points: two countries can both score high while one agrees and the other disagrees. Switch the cells to Share who agree below to see which way each majority points.

Do language models share our common sense?

People don't always agree on what common sense is. If there is no “ground truth” to common sense, how do we evaluate large language models? This study provides a solution. We recruited — people and used — LLMs to rate a total of — statements. The patterns of agreement in their ratings determine their common sense. Explore the main findings here.

4 · Why it matters

LLM common sense is typically evaluated using static benchmarks that come with predefined ground-truth answers. But people often disagree with one another on what counts as ground truth. Our study offers a novel methodology to examine the common sense of humans and models without this assumption, highlighting the empirical, collective-agreement nature of commonsense knowledge in society.
